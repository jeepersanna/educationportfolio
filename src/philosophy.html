Philosophy of education

What I believe about how kids learn, and what schools owe them and their families.

Where it comes from

I learned at home first

Every child who walks into a classroom arrives with a history: knowledge from home, from community, from the years that came before. My job as their teacher is to see that history, take it seriously, and build on it.

I was homeschooled through high school, which gave me real freedom but also made me acutely aware of what absence of structure can cost. I didn't set foot in a formal classroom until community college. Those experiences taught me that quality public education is a right, not a privilege, and its absence leaves marks.

Later, as a parent, I spent years helping run a parent co-op forest school and a weekly play group. When a kid was struggling, our families added support instead of pushing them out. Now I bring those lessons to my kids' public school.

Commitments

What I believe

1

Culture is the core, not an add-on

Culture is the operating system through which students process new learning. I bring an ethnic studies perspective to every subject area: whose knowledge is centered, whose stories are told, and what the lesson assumes about who belongs in the room.

2

Trust makes rigor possible

Trust is not separate from demanding academic work; it is what makes rigor possible. That means safety, belonging, and a classroom students feel is theirs.

3

One bar, many paths

I design for the full range of learners actually in the room: students reading below grade level and above it, students acquiring English, students with IEPs. The scaffolds shift in form but not in expectation.

4

Students do the thinking

Students do the intellectual work instead of watching me do it. Writing has a real destination, like opinion letters to decision-makers, because 3rd through 5th graders are fully capable of advocacy, and most of them have never been asked.

5

Curiosity, not removal

When a child's behavior disrupts the classroom, I respond with curiosity rather than removal. Children don't dysregulate in a vacuum. There is a need that is unmet, and removal addresses the disruption without touching the cause.

6

Families are first teachers

Families are a child's first and most enduring teachers. They know their child in ways I never will. I reach out early and often and treat what families know as essential rather than supplemental.
