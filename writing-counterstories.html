Essay · Families, rights, and belonging

The ways U.S.-born citizens talk back against the anchor baby narrative

LALS 1, The Latinx Experience in the U.S., CCSF · Summer 2026

The anchor baby narrative is incredibly derogatory towards immigrant families, and it’s totally unfounded once you check it against immigration law, deportation rates, and data on immigrant families (Rodriguez 99). Still, the citizens at the center of it are not silent. Rodriguez calls what they do "talking back," and she catalogs a range of responses, from claims about an ancestral right to land to arguments about the Constitution, racism, and morality (Rodriguez 98). In the language of the class, these are counterstories that challenge what she calls a "majoritarian mind-set," the assumptions the dominant group brings to conversations about race (Rodriguez 99).

One common response is that this is a nation of immigrants. Many of the people who use the word "anchor baby" are only a few generations removed from being called something similar. Rodriguez notes that participants point out that Donald Trump, or his children, would count as anchor babies under the same logic (Rodriguez 98). The point is simple: if one citizen’s belonging is up for debate, then everyone’s should be, other than Native Americans. The history runs deeper than that. Before the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, much of the United States was Mexico, and before that it was Native land. The US government made these people strangers in their own homes through legal violence. As one person in the book puts it, "A lot of people forget that the US has taken their lands historically … History is still repeating itself" (Rodriguez 96). That history is a counterstory on its own, and it exposes what the label really does. Rodriguez describes these children as citizens "whose citizenship is suspect, if not denied, on account of the racialized identity of her immigrant ancestry" (Rodriguez 104). The tag racializes Latinx people as permanently foreign, no matter what their papers say.

Another response names the problem as manufactured. Rodriguez traces it to two laws, the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 and the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, which militarized the border and cut off the seasonal migration Mexican workers had relied on for years. Those laws "made border crossing more dangerous and permanent settlement more likely" (Rodriguez 7). So the permanence nativists now call an invasion is something US policy created, which moves the blame off of families and onto the government.

The strongest pushback takes apart the logic of the term. The idea is that a citizen child is a shortcut to papers for the parents. In reality, parents can be deported long before a child turns twenty-one, the age required to sponsor them, and even then the process is costly and can trigger a bar on reentry (Rodriguez 76, 107). Rodriguez draws the line clearly: there is "a difference in parents wanting opportunity for their children versus parents using children as legal or economic pawns" (Rodriguez 98). Tomás makes it concrete. His uncle had three US citizen children and was deported anyway. Thinking of his cousins, who now grow up without their father, he asks, "How are they anchor babies?" (Rodriguez 107). The data backs him up: "From 2005 to 2013, the United States deported over 3 million migrants, which affected over 1.5 million citizen children" (Rodriguez 100). A country deporting the parents of that many citizen children is not being anchored down. Those children are punished for their parents’ status.

By telling these stories, the people in Rodriguez’s study refuse to let others define their families. That is the power of the counterstory.

Works cited

  • Rodriguez, Cassaundra. Contested Americans: Mixed-Status Families in Anti-Immigrant Times. New York University Press, 2023.
