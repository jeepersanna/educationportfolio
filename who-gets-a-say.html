Work/Research

Who gets a say?

Structural barriers to family participation in a multilingual public school.

Completed
Course
SOC 1 Honors, CCSF, Spring 2026
Site
A multilingual public elementary school in San Francisco
Methods
12 semi-structured interviews with parents and community members, thematic analysis

The question

Public schools are among the few civic institutions where working-class and immigrant families hold formal rights to take part in governance. In California, ELAC and School Site Councils are meant to give parents a seat at the table on budget, program, and policy decisions. On paper, these structures democratize school governance. In practice, it’s more complicated.

I asked who actually gets a say in how the school is run, what makes meaningful participation possible or hard, and what it would take to make it equitable.

How I did it

I interviewed 12 parents and community members connected to one multilingual public elementary school, chosen for variation in language background, race and ethnicity, class, formal role, and years at the school. Some held roles on the PTA, SSC, or ELAC, and some held none. Primary languages included English, Spanish, and Cantonese. Interviews ran 30 to 60 minutes in April and May 2026. I read each interview for themes, then read across all twelve for patterns and for places where experiences differed.

What I found

  • Formal structures, uneven participation. ELAC, SSC, and PTA function as consultative rather than participatory bodies. Families are included in school life more than in school decisions.
  • Language as hierarchy, not just barrier. Even with translation, families who aren’t English-dominant are a step behind in the conversation.
  • Informal bridge workers. A few bilingual parents do translation and connection work that is essential, unpaid, unrecognized, and fragile.
  • How meetings signal who belongs. Whoever raises an idea is expected to carry it out, and meeting times favor families already inside.
  • Citizenship as an unnamed filter. For mixed-status families, engaging with school leadership can carry real risk, and that is rarely acknowledged.
  • What works. Participation is real when communication is a conversation, when multilingual families are the primary audience from the start, and when the school’s family liaisons have the support to do their work.

What it means

Formal structures exist but fall short. Whose voice gets heard is shaped by language, race, class, social connections, and citizenship status, none of which are equally distributed. The families whose children would benefit most from equitable governance are among those most often left out.

That isn’t because families don’t care. Not one person I interviewed suggested that. Multilingual families’ limited voice in decisions reflects a failure of institutional design, not a lack of interest or effort. The school also isn’t failing: people described real warmth for its staff and family liaisons alongside their critique.

Limits and next steps

  • One school community, so the findings can’t be generalized beyond it.
  • The families most disconnected from school are the hardest to reach, so they are likely underrepresented.
  • All interviews were in English, which almost certainly undersampled the people most affected by language barriers.
  • Next, I’d like to observe meetings directly, compare schools with different language demographics, and use community-based methods where families help shape the study as co-investigators.

I’m now turning what I learned into a free set of meeting tools, which I’m trying out this year: name tags that show your language, norms for small groups first, and response cards for families who’d rather not speak up in the room.
