Peace as a direction

HIST 17B, US History, CCSF · Summer 2026

I have stopped thinking of peace as a destination and started thinking of it as a direction. The world I want may never fully arrive in my lifetime. A world without war or genocide or hate. A world that has done away with the cage and the surveillance camera, that guarantees every person a home, a meal, and a doctor, that opens its borders to the natural rhythm of people who move with the seasons. Equality and equity for everyone. A cared-for Earth. An economy built for people rather than for the hoarding of wealth. These are not items on a list I expect to check off. They are the horizon I steer by, the fixed points that tell me whether I am walking in the right direction.

This course taught me how that direction holds. Rights written on paper mean nothing until people are willing to enforce them. Every movement worth studying left someone out, so I have learned to ask, always, who is missing. Education is a condition of freedom rather than a luxury. Real change alters the conditions that cause harm instead of soothing the symptom. And the question of who gets to tell the story is never neutral. It is a question of power.

My own road is smaller than the horizon, and that is as it should be. It begins where I already stand, in my community: the Mission, the Ingleside, the Excelsior, and the public schools that hold their children and their teachers. From there I have to choose a direction, and this year is for choosing honestly. I want to know how a degree in Communication Design can serve the people around me, whether my path runs through teaching or through research, and I will spend the year taking classes and asking those who have walked ahead of me before I commit to a program. Once I choose, I will earn my master’s and learn to teach. Then I will do the work itself, rooted in community, whether that means a classroom, a tutoring table, a webpage built for a neighborhood, or an organizing meeting.

And I will keep returning to the young. Children need somewhere they are allowed to be fully themselves. Teenagers need a safe place to test their independence, to build honest relationships, to stand in the natural world, to feel useful and to feel that they belong. Youth are the future, always, and we will not have a better world until we begin treating them like the important people they already are.

I know the obstacles by name. Indecision, which I will meet by trying things rather than waiting for certainty. The cost of graduate school, which I will meet with grants, scholarships, and a plan. Burnout, which I will meet by building rest into the work and asking for help, because no one does this alone. The feeling of starting late, which is really the fact of arriving experienced, since every year I have lived is fuel for better teaching. And the loneliness of the work, which I will answer with study partners, mutual aid, and organizing.

None of this finishes the horizon. It was never meant to. It moves me toward it, and it teaches the next people to keep walking after me.
