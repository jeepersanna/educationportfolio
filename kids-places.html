Work/Research/Where Do Kids Get to Just Exist?

Where do kids get to just exist?

Parents’ memories of free places, and their children’s independence in San Francisco.

In progress · round one done
Course
SOC 21, Introduction to Research Methods, CCSF, Fall 2026
Site
San Francisco families
Methods
3 semi-structured interviews with parents, coded by theme. Next: a parent survey.

The question

Kids in American cities have lost many of the places where they could spend time without an adult watching, a schedule to follow, or a price of admission. Researchers call the freedom to get around without an adult independent mobility, and it has been shrinking for decades as cities were built around cars, organized activities replaced free play, and parents grew more worried about safety.

Parents sit in the middle of that change. They remember the places they had, and they decide which places their own kids get. I asked: How do parents raising kids in a city describe the places they had outside home and school growing up, and how do those compare to the places their kids have now?

How I did it

In October 2026 I interviewed three parents raising kids in San Francisco, each for 30 to 45 minutes. I chose parents who grew up in very different places, a ranching town, a suburb, and a small industrial city, so that where they grew up could serve as a point of comparison. I asked about the places they spent time as kids and teens, who was watching, what cost money, and then about their own kids now: where they go, what parents are comfortable with, what worries them, and what would need to change. I took handwritten notes, coded them, and grouped the codes into four themes and one thread that ran through all of them.

What I found

  • Then vs. now is not one story. Two parents had far more unsupervised freedom than their kids have. The third had less. She was kept within earshot as a child and closely monitored as a teen, and now her kids ride the bus, walk to the store, and go to the library with friends. A city with transit gave them more range than she had in a small town.
  • Cheap places went away. Parents remembered cheap movie theaters, friends’ houses, and cheap gas. Those places closed or got expensive. One parent worried that kids out on their own are treated as a nuisance by other adults.
  • Freedom comes from preparation, not age. None of the parents tied independence to a set age. They answer fear with practice, route planning, and smart watches instead of keeping kids home. That work takes parent time, which not every family has.
  • What kids miss is making their own fun. Asked what their kids gain or miss, all three described self-directed time: being bored, coming up with ideas, cooking and building things, moving their bodies, without a program or a screen.
  • The thread: getting around. The parents’ own teen freedom ran on cars. Their kids get range on foot, by scooter, and on buses and BART. What parents want in place is mostly about getting around safely.

What it means

Most of this fits the research: each generation of parents measures their kids’ lives against their own freedom, and parents’ sense of safety matters more than how much play space a neighborhood has. The exception matters. One parent’s childhood was the restricted one, because her parents didn’t trust their neighborhood. That suggests restriction depends on a family’s relationship to the place around it, not just on the decade, and it pushes against the idea that small-town kids always had more freedom.

If fear matters more than play space, the question becomes what changes fear. These parents pointed to things a city can build: safe routes, known safe stops, and transit kids can learn. Public money for kids’ time outside school mostly pays for supervision. What these parents say is missing is unsupervised time, and safe places to spend it.

Limits and next steps

  • Three parents from my own networks, all raising kids in the same city, so the findings can’t stand for a larger trend.
  • Notes were handwritten, so most of the data is paraphrase.
  • Every account here is an adult’s view of a child’s life.
  • Next: a survey. A survey of San Francisco parents will show how common these patterns are across more families, and test what actually changes parents’ fears, including whether other adults’ judgment shapes what they allow.
  • Later: more parent interviews across a wider range of incomes and places parents grew up, and mapping where kids in the Excelsior and Ingleside actually spend unsupervised time.

This builds on my Ocean Avenue café study, which asked what makes a third place for adults. This project asks the same question about kids.

Interviews are confidential, so the full paper isn’t posted here. I’m happy to talk about the study.
