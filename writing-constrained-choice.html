Constrained choice: sterilization and Americanization in Puerto Rico

LALS 1, The Latinx Experience in the U.S., CCSF · Summer 2026

One idea does the heavy lifting across this whole module. The colonizing mission reached past roads, sugar contracts, and English classes and went after consciousness itself. Cabán argues that Americanization set out to build "a new and alien worldview divorced from the historical context of the Puerto Rican people’s lived experiences" (121). Once you take that seriously, sterilization stops looking like a separate horror and starts looking like the same project reaching the body.

The economic story sets it up. After the U.S. dispossessed the jíbaro and rebuilt the island as a sugar monoculture, the 1929 crash left landless workers with nothing, and administrators relabeled them an "excess population" (Mojica). That phrase is doing eugenic work. It treats poverty as a trait of inferior people rather than a result of colonial policy, which is the same move Race: The Power of an Illusion traced when eugenics was used to predict the disappearance of African Americans. Blame the bodies, not the system that made them poor.

Here is where I want to be careful, because the obvious reading is also the one Briggs warns against. The lecture lays out two interpretations: women were forced, or Americanization made sterilization feel rational, with propaganda tying big families to poverty and small families to prosperity (Mojica). Mojica leans toward the second. So do I, but Briggs pushes me to hold it loosely. She finds no evidence of an organized coercive campaign, and she points out that mainland feminists who cast Puerto Rican women as helpless victims "inadvertently recapitulated the terms of a U.S. colonialist narrative" (Briggs 159). That stopped me. You can erase a woman’s agency in the name of defending her. Following Iris López, Briggs calls it "constrained choice," which names both the real coercion of the shop-floor clinics and the fact that women were still making decisions inside a trap they did not build (159).

That framing connects straight back to Module 5. The colonial labor system segmented work by race and class, and here it reaches into reproduction. Textile and canning plants targeted women, maternity leave cost money, and so sterilization on the company floor became, in the lecture’s blunt terms, the cheapest way to exploit poor women’s bodies (Mojica). Profit set the terms of motherhood.

Citizenship is the part I find hardest to sit with. The Jones Act made Puerto Ricans citizens in 1917, but island residents still cannot vote for president or send a voting member to Congress, and within months of the grant they were eligible for the draft and sent to World War I (Mojica). Cabán reads the Act as a way "to make clear that Porto Rico is to remain permanently connected with the United States," not as a gift of rights (qtd. in Cabán 136). Citizenship enough to die for the country, not enough to vote in it.

Consent is the hardest piece. A power can make people choose what it wants and still call the choice freely made. That is the most durable thing Americanization built, and it is what makes this history feel less finished than it should.
