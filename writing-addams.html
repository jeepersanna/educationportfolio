Relief or reform: Jane Addams and Hull-House

HIST 17B, US History, CCSF · Summer 2026

Jane Addams never treated relief as enough. She names the limit of charity: "private beneficence is totally inadequate to deal with the vast numbers of the city’s disinherited." Charity can feed a family tonight, but it can’t touch why they’re hungry. For her, the real work was reform, changing the conditions that keep people poor.

What surprised me is how she got to the root of social problems: she lived in community. She moved into Hull House and stayed in the neighborhood, instead of helping from a distance. That closeness changed what she could see and do. It turned into real reforms, "the most significant suggestions for eradicating poverty" that became factory-safety laws and child labor limits. And it built genuine care, because, as she wrote, "social sentiments... live only by communion and fellowship, [and] are cultivated most easily in the fostering soil of a community life." The reform and the care came from the same choice: to live there.

That shifts how I think about wanting my own work to matter, especially with young people and underserved communities. It’s easy to feel useful handing out help. But Addams pushes the harder question: am I soothing the symptom, or changing what makes the help necessary? Relief that never turns into reform leaves the structure standing.
